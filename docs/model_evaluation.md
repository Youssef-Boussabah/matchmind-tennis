# Model Evaluation

How the from-scratch decision tree and random forest were configured and scored on historical
ATP matches, against scikit-learn reference implementations and two transparent baselines.

Reproduce with:

```bash
uv run python scripts/build_dataset.py
uv run python scripts/evaluate_models.py
```

The machine-readable results are written to `results/test_metrics.csv` and
`results/validation_results.csv`.

---

## Design

**Dataset.** `data/processed/match_features.csv` — 99,158 ATP matches from 1991 to a partial
2026, generated by replaying the yearly Jeff Sackmann match files forward in date order. Each
row carries 8 metadata columns, the 67 model features and the binary target `RESULT`.

SHA-256 `22425a372212c475ff0eacaf7a85916cc2468d16a504641ad06883754ddbe1ef`.

**Evaluation window.** 98,013 of those matches — everything through the end of 2025 — are
eligible for evaluation. The remaining **1,145 rows, 20260104 to 20260517, are excluded**: the
2026 season is only recorded as far as May, so it is a clay-court stretch rather than a year,
and scoring on it would measure the calendar rather than the model. Those rows take no part in
fitting, selection or scoring. They are still in the dataset because the deployment model reads
the whole history; they are simply outside every period.

**Features.** Exactly the 67 columns in `FEATURE_NAMES`, in that order — differences between
player 1 and player 2: Elo overall and per surface, ATP rank and points, age and height,
matches played, head-to-head overall and per surface, Elo gradient and win count over seven
rolling windows, and six serve statistics over the same windows, plus `BEST_OF` and
`DRAW_SIZE`.

The metadata columns — `tourney_date`, `tourney_id`, `match_num`, `tourney_name`, `surface`,
`round`, `player1_id`, `player2_id` — are never model inputs. They identify a match; they are
not evidence about who wins it.

**Periods.** Cut on `tourney_date`, never shuffled:

| Partition | Period | Matches | First | Last | Player 1 wins |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Train | 1991-01-01 to 2021-12-31 | 86,892 | 19910107 | 20211205 | 50.07% |
| Validation | 2022-01-01 to 2023-12-31 | 5,532 | 20220103 | 20231127 | 50.33% |
| Test | 2024-01-01 to 2025-12-31 | 5,589 | 20240101 | 20251217 | 50.04% |
| *Excluded* | *2026, partial season* | *1,145* | *20260104* | *20260517* | *52.14%* |

86,892 + 5,532 + 5,589 = 98,013 — every eligible match, nothing dropped and nothing shared.
Every match on a given tournament date lands in the same partition, because the cut is on the
date itself.

The periods are cut on `tourney_date`, not on which yearly file a row came from. The 2025 file
opens on 2024-12-27, so 81 of its matches carry a 2024 date and belong to the test period; that
is the chronologically correct home for them, and the test set is not "the 2024 file plus the
2025 file".

**Selection.** Every configuration was fitted on train and scored on validation. The lowest
**validation log loss** in each family decided the configuration; accuracy was a secondary
sanity check. Log loss was chosen because it scores the probability a model actually outputs
rather than only which side of 0.5 it fell on.

**Test firewall.** The window above and the candidate list below were fixed before any refreshed
number was seen — the window during a read-only audit of the data, the candidates unchanged from
the previous evaluation. Only after every choice was frozen were the selected configurations
refitted on train + validation (92,424 matches, all eligible history through the end of 2023)
and scored once on the test period. **No test metric was consulted before a choice was made, and
nothing was retuned afterwards.**

## Baselines

Both are hard-label rules, so only accuracy is reported. Neither produces a probability, and
inventing one would make the log loss and Brier columns say more about the transformation than
about the rule.

| Baseline | Rule | Validation | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Majority | Always predict the more common class in the fitting data | 0.5033 | 0.5004 |
| ATP rank | Predict the better-ranked player; ties to player 2 | 0.6394 | 0.6411 |

The majority baseline sits at chance, as it should: player 1 and player 2 are assigned by a
fair coin when the dataset is built, so there is no side to be biased towards.

The rank baseline is the one that matters. ATP rank alone gets 64.1% on the test period, which
is most of what any model here achieves. Every claim below has to be read against that number,
not against 50%.

## Decision tree

Four configurations, `max_features=None`, `random_state=0`, fitted on train and scored on
validation:

| max_depth | min_samples_split | Custom log loss | Custom accuracy | sklearn log loss | sklearn accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 20 | 0.6220 | 0.6399 | 0.6220 | 0.6399 |
| **6** | **20** | **0.6200** | 0.6437 | **0.6202** | 0.6437 |
| 8 | 20 | 0.6347 | 0.6433 | 0.6288 | 0.6435 |
| 8 | 50 | 0.6355 | 0.6433 | 0.6286 | 0.6443 |

**Both implementations selected `max_depth=6, min_samples_split=20`,** agreeing on the winner
and on validation accuracy to six decimal places, with log losses 0.0002 apart.

Depth 6 wins on both metrics at once here, so there is nothing to arbitrate — but the reason
depth 8 loses is still worth naming, because it is the same effect as before. A depth-6 tree's
leaves stay large enough to hold both outcomes: its validation probabilities span 0.067 to 0.941
and never reach a certainty. The depth-8 trees reach leaves small enough to be unanimous and
put 7 validation matches (3 at `min_samples_split=50`) on an exact 0.0 or 1.0. A leaf that
claims certainty is unboundedly wrong when it misses, which is what the log-loss column is
reacting to while accuracy barely moves.

This is a change from the previous evaluation, which selected depth 4 on the 1991–2022 window.
Nothing about the candidate list or the metric changed; the extra season of training data moved
the winner one level deeper.

## Random forest

`max_features="sqrt"`, `bootstrap=True`, `random_state=0`:

| n_estimators | max_depth | min_samples_split | Custom log loss | Custom accuracy | sklearn log loss | sklearn accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | 6 | 20 | 0.6178 | 0.6473 | 0.6157 | 0.6513 |
| **25** | **8** | **20** | **0.6122** | 0.6555 | 0.6137 | 0.6571 |
| 50 | 8 | 20 | 0.6127 | 0.6537 | **0.6132** | 0.6564 |

**The two implementations selected different configurations here.** The custom forest chose
`n_estimators=25, max_depth=8, min_samples_split=20`; scikit-learn's chose the same depth with
50 trees. Both agree that depth 8 is right and that depth 6 is not.

The disagreement is not meaningful. It rests on 0.0005 of log loss for the custom forest (0.6122
against 0.6127) and 0.0005 for scikit-learn's in the other direction (0.6132 against 0.6137).
Two forests with different bootstrap draws and different feature subsets landing on opposite
sides of a gap that small is what noise looks like, not a finding about tree counts. The honest
reading is that 25 and 50 trees perform the same on this data and the search is well into
diminishing returns — which is also why there was no case for trying 100 or 500.

It does have one concrete consequence: the deployment model is the custom forest, so it is
configured from the custom forest's own winning row, **25 trees**. The scikit-learn result is a
reference implementation and does not configure what ships.

Depth 8 helps a forest as much as it hurt a single tree: averaging 25 trees smooths away the
overconfident leaves that made the deep single tree's log loss so poor.

This too is a change from the previous evaluation, which selected 50 trees on the older window.

## Final test results

2024-2025, 5,589 matches, each model refitted on train + validation (92,424 matches) and scored
exactly once.

| Model | Implementation | Accuracy | Log loss | Brier | ROC AUC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Majority baseline | — | 0.500447 | — | — | — |
| ATP rank heuristic | — | 0.641081 | — | — | — |
| Decision tree | from scratch | 0.636608 | 0.626537 | 0.218929 | 0.701394 |
| Decision tree | scikit-learn | 0.636608 | 0.626423 | 0.218879 | 0.701576 |
| Random forest | from scratch | 0.646985 | 0.618250 | 0.215243 | 0.712438 |
| Random forest | scikit-learn | **0.649490** | **0.617280** | **0.214884** | **0.713367** |

The two decision-tree implementations produce identical held-out **accuracy** to six decimal
places and log losses 0.0001 apart, which is strong evidence that the custom implementation
behaves equivalently to scikit-learn's on this task. It is behavioural evidence, not structural:
the internal nodes and thresholds of the two trees were not compared, so nothing here shows they
are the same tree. (The custom split search *is* separately tested for exact equality against a
slow reference implementation of the same rule, on synthetic data — that is what pins down its
internals.)

The forests differ slightly more, which is expected, and here they are not even running the same
configuration: 25 trees against 50, because they picked different winners on validation. They
still land within 0.0025 accuracy and 0.001 log loss of each other — close enough that which one
comes out ahead on any given metric should not be read as a difference between the
implementations. No paired test of that difference was run either.

Both decision trees score *below* the ATP rank heuristic on this test period (0.6366 against
0.6411). That is reported as measured; the forests remain above it.

## Runtime

Final models fitted on 92,424 rows, 67 features:

| Model | Fit (s) | Predict on 5,589 rows (s) |
|---|---|---|
| Decision tree, from scratch | 3.18 | 0.007 |
| Decision tree, scikit-learn | 2.12 | 0.001 |
| Random forest, from scratch (25 trees) | 18.66 | 0.225 |
| Random forest, scikit-learn (50 trees) | 10.41 | 0.019 |

The two forest rows are not a like-for-like timing comparison — they run different tree counts,
because that is what each implementation selected. Per tree, the from-scratch forest is roughly
3.5x slower.

The whole evaluation — 14 candidate fits plus 4 final fits — takes about 142 seconds. Building
the dataset from the raw yearly files takes about 78 seconds.

The from-scratch models are 1.5x to 3.5x slower than scikit-learn's, which is a reasonable place
for pure NumPy to land against compiled Cython. The gap is a constant factor, not a difference
in complexity.

## Probability metrics

Accuracy answers only whether the model picked the right side. The other three say something
about the probability itself:

- **Log loss** punishes confident mistakes hardest. It is the primary metric here.
- **Brier score** is the mean squared error of the probability. A model that always said 0.5
  would score 0.25; the forest scores 0.2152.
- **ROC AUC** is the chance a randomly chosen player-1 win is given a higher probability than a
  randomly chosen player-1 loss. At 0.7124 the forest ranks matches meaningfully better than a
  coin flip (0.5) while being far from separable (1.0).

Log loss is computed on probabilities clipped to `[1e-15, 1 - 1e-15]`, because it is infinite at
an exactly wrong certainty and a depth-limited tree does produce leaves at 0.0 and 1.0.
Accuracy, Brier and ROC AUC use the models' unmodified output.

**The probabilities are not calibrated.** No calibration step was fitted, and none of the
numbers above should be read as a claim that a predicted 0.7 means seventy percent.

scikit-learn impurity-based feature importances, from the two reference models. These are a

Four strength measures — Elo, surface Elo, ATP points and ATP rank — account for about 60% of

No importance is reported for the from-scratch models: they do not implement it, and adding an
importance API to fill in a table would be the wrong reason to add one.

## Interpretation

The from-scratch random forest predicts 64.7% of ATP matches in 2024-2025 correctly, having
never seen a match from those years during training or configuration selection.

What that is worth depends on the comparison:

- Against **chance** (50.0%) it is a large gain, but chance is a weak bar here by construction.
- Against the **ATP rank heuristic** (0.641081) it is a gain of about **0.59 percentage points —
  33 matches out of 5,589**. That is a small observed margin. The two predictors score the same
  matches, so establishing whether the difference is real would need a paired test; none was run,
  and **no significance claim is made here in either direction**.
- Against the **scikit-learn forest** (64.9%) it is close, which is the point.

That margin over the rank heuristic has narrowed against the previous evaluation, where it was
about 1.2 points on 2023-2024. Two things moved at once and the design cannot separate them: the
test period changed, and the rank heuristic itself did better on the new one (64.1% against
63.6%). The forest's own accuracy is close to unchanged. The plainest description is that
2024-2025 was a more rank-predictable pair of seasons, which lifts the baseline more than it
lifts the model. Nothing was retuned in response.

The honest summary is that the ranking system already encodes most of what is predictable about
a tennis match from pre-match information, and that Elo, surface Elo and recent form add a small
amount on top. That is consistent with what published work on tennis prediction generally finds,
and a result far above this would be a reason to go looking for leakage rather than to
celebrate.

The models are comfortably better than the baselines on the probability metrics, where the
hard-label baselines cannot compete at all — a rule that only ever says "player 1" or "player 2"
has nothing to offer anyone who needs to know how sure it is.

## The shipped model is not this model

The numbers above come from a model deliberately kept ignorant of 2024-2025.

The configuration selected for the from-scratch forest — `n_estimators=25, max_depth=8,
min_samples_split=20`, with `max_features="sqrt"`, `bootstrap=True`, `min_impurity_decrease=0.0`
and `random_state=0` — is now frozen in `scripts/build_prediction_bundle.py`. To be explicit
about the order things happened in:

- selection ran on the **2022-2023 validation period only**;
- **2024-2025 was untouched** until every setting was fixed, and was then scored once;
- **2026 was excluded entirely** because the season is partial;
- after the evaluation was closed, the deployment model was refit with that same configuration
  on **all 99,158 canonical matches**, including the excluded 2026 rows, so that predictions
  about a future match use the most recent retained history available.

That last refit is **not an evaluation result**. It produces the right artifact to predict with
and the wrong one to quote performance from: it has seen every match it could be scored against.
Its training-set accuracy is not a result and is not reported anywhere. Nothing in the tables
above was measured on a model that had trained on the rows it was scored on.

`models/matchmind_v1_bundle.json.gz` is that artifact, built from this dataset:

| | |
|---|---|
| Implementation | `matchmind_v1.trees.RandomForest` (from scratch) |
| Configuration | 25 trees, `max_depth=8`, `min_samples_split=20`, `max_features="sqrt"`, `bootstrap=True`, `min_impurity_decrease=0.0`, `random_state=0` |
| Training rows | 99,158 — the whole canonical history |
| History replayed to | 20260517 |
| Rated players | 1,968 |
| Profiles | 1,968, every one resolving to a name |
| SHA-256 | `2b97e793fc946bf27b1a024ab46608264d1623d5927b6db21fd43d7ce9b22d24` |
| Size | 3,997,928 bytes |

The build was reproduced byte-for-byte in the validated project environment by building a second
copy and comparing it.

Note the two dates that are easy to confuse. The raw source files reach `tourney_date` 20260525,
but all 127 Roland Garros 2026 rows lack the serve statistics the feature engine requires and are
dropped by the cleaner, so the canonical history — and therefore the deployment state — ends at
**20260517**.

## Limitations

- **Tournament dates, not match timestamps.** `tourney_date` is the tournament start date rather
  than a per-match timestamp, and every match in an event shares it. Within an event the replay
  uses `match_num` plus the source tie-breakers; `round` is also carried by the source but is not
  part of the sort, so the resulting order is deterministic rather than an exact played-at
  chronology. Two matches on the same day of the same event may therefore have been replayed in a
  different order than they were played. The period boundaries are unaffected, but the
  within-tournament state is approximate.
- **Evaluation stops at the end of 2025.** The history runs into 2026, but that season is
  recorded only as far as May, so its 1,145 matches are excluded from train, validation and test
  alike. They are not a held-out year and are never reported as one.
- **13,610 matches were dropped** as missing required fields or physically impossible, mostly
  serve statistics missing in the early 1990s. Coverage in the source is not uniform across the
  period, so the training data is not a uniform sample of ATP tennis.
- **No injury, odds, weather or fatigue inputs.** The features describe two players' recorded
  history and the match format. Retirements and walkovers appear as completed results with no
  marker distinguishing them.
- **Probabilities are not calibrated.** Treat them as scores that rank matches sensibly, not as
  literal win probabilities.
- **One test period.** 5,589 matches over two seasons is a single sample of one era. The numbers
  would move if the boundary moved, and no confidence interval is claimed for any of them. The
  forest's margin over the ATP rank heuristic on this period is a small 0.59 points; no paired
  test was run, so it is reported as an observed margin and not as an established gain.
